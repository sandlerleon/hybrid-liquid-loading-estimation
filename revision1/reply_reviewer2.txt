Thank you for the detailed review, which led to substantial new analyses. I cannot supply loop data, so the revision tests the assumptions the conclusions depend on and narrows the claims where the tests warrant it; the answers are numbered as in the review. The full response is in the uploaded file FMI_Response_to_Reviewers_R1.docx.

R2.1. Lack of experimental validation
Response: Experimental validation is not available to me: I am an independent researcher without a wet-gas flow loop, and the manuscript says throughout that no loop data were used. What the revision can do, and does, is to justify the assumed values, to show how far the conclusions depend on them, and to state the limits plainly. The 20% is related to the specified calibration (Section 3.3.1): it is a performance requirement, equivalent to a calibrated 90% interval of ±33%, that a fitted estimator must meet. Section 5.11 varies the assumption from 10% to 60%, the transfer bias from 0 to 20%, a persistent bias at one site from −20% to +20%, and the carryover prevalence from 0 to 60%, each with the declared uncertainty either matched or left at the assumed value. Table 10 separates what the simulations demonstrate from what is proposed, and the Limitations (Section 6.4) are extended. The conclusions that depend on the assumptions are stated as conditional: the diagnostic estimator alone is the best choice when it is better than assumed, and fusion helps when it is worse.
Change: Sections 3.3.1, 5.11 (new), 6.3, 6.4; Table 10 (new); Fig. 10.

R2.2. Potential circularity of the uncertainty assessment
Response: Accepted. Section 4.5 (new) separates the correction model (always Chisholm) from the model that generates the simulated reading, and Section 5.12 (new) evaluates three situations: matched, an independent published orifice correlation (Murdock, 1962), and systematic (±5–10%) and random (5–10%) departures of the over-reading, the latter as stress scenarios and not as error bounds. With the independent correlation the oracle band rises from ±1.82% to ±2.58% and the full method from ±4.26% to ±5.26% (bias +1.19%); the realized coverage of the declared ±2u interval falls from 93.6% to 88.5%, and a declared model-form allowance of about 15% of the over-reading is needed to restore 95%. The ranking of methods also changes with the sign of the correlation error, and the apparent advantage of the full method when the over-reading is overstated is identified as compensation of errors. The model-form term u_form is now part of the uncertainty budget (Eq. 16), and the text states that the allowance restoring coverage is specific to the correlations, geometry and conditions tested and must be calibrated for each application. The Limitations are rewritten accordingly.
Change: Sections 3.5.2, 4.5, 5.12 (new), 6.4; Table 8; Fig. 11; Eq. (16).

R2.3. Justification of the asymmetric weighting rule
Response: Section 3.5.1 gives the physical justification (a carryover fraction cannot be negative, so only an upward excess of the diagnostic estimate is evidence of carryover) and the statistical one (the damping factor is the Cauchy influence weight of iteratively reweighted least squares, applied to the excess beyond k). Section 5.10 and Table 7 compare Eq. (12) at k = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 3 with symmetric weighting, a Huber weight, a two-sided Cauchy weight, a one-sided hard switch at four thresholds, and the thermodynamic and diagnostic estimators alone, at carryover prevalences of 10%, 25% and 40%. The results qualify the choice of the first version: k = 1 is not the best threshold in the reference case (±4.30% against ±4.01% at k = 0.5), the hard one-sided switch is narrower than Eq. (12) at all three prevalences (±3.49% against ±4.30% at 25%) but poorer when carryover is absent, and the two-sided control is poorer than the one-sided rule. No rule is uniformly best. Eq. (12) is presented as a smooth, continuous baseline and not as the preferred implementation; the hard switch is described as a promising alternative that needs independent validation, especially of its switching threshold, and the choice is a calibration question for loop data.
Change: Sections 3.5, 3.5.1 (new), 5.10 (new), 6.4; Table 7 (new).

R2.4. Validation of the consistency index
Response: Table 4 now reports, for every threshold, the accepted fraction, the 95% error band of the accepted realizations, sensitivity, specificity, precision and false-positive rate, and Section 5.9 (new) adds the ROC and precision–recall curves (Fig. 9, AUC 0.81), the detection rate against the size of the carryover, and a second target, a corrected-rate error above 3%. At Z_max = 3 the specificity is 99.95% and the false-positive rate 0.05%, but the sensitivity is 2.9%; at Z_max = 1 the sensitivity is 71% with a false-positive rate of 21%. Z_max = 3 is justified only as a completeness-priority setting that removes gross disagreement, and Section 5.4 now states what a lower threshold costs. The result is less favorable than the first version suggested, and the text is changed: the index is described as a gross-disagreement flag with high precision and low sensitivity, not as a carryover detector.
Change: Sections 5.3, 5.4, 5.9 (new); Table 4; Fig. 9.

R2.5. Uncertainty propagation
Response: Section 3.5.2 (new) gives the propagation: first-order, in the form u²(Q_G)/Q_G² = sᵀΣs (Eq. 15), which for the uncorrelated groups of inputs becomes Eq. (16). The estimator uncertainties and their correlation enter through u_f, which carries c = ρ₁₂u₁u₂; the meter terms are lumped (0.7%) and itemized in Table 6; the correlation residual (0.6%) and a model-form term u_form complete the budget. The one correlation not treated, between the liquid-loading estimate and the gas-density term, is bounded: with a correlation of ±1 the combined uncertainty at X_LM = 0.05 would lie between 1.06% and 1.32% instead of 1.19% (Table 6). The realized coverage of the declared ±2u interval is 93.6% in the reference case (Section 5.12), and the propagation of the liquid-loading term was checked against Monte Carlo propagation (verify_revision.py, check V8).
Change: Sections 3.5, 3.5.1, 3.5.2 (new); Eqs. (13)–(16); Table 6; Section 5.12.

R2.7. Numerical consistency
Response: The two figures are both correct and refer to different populations: ±4.28% is the asymmetric weighting over all 300,000 realizations, and ±4.26% is the full method over the 99.2% of realizations that the acceptance test retains. The Abstract and Section 5.2 now state both and explain that they differ by less than the sampling error of the band (0.015 percentage points), so the acceptance test does not measurably narrow the band at Z_max = 3. The phrase “95% uncertainty band” is replaced by “95% error band” (see comment 10).
Change: Abstract; Section 5.2.

R2.8. Diagnostic uncertainty
Response: A new paragraph in Section 3.3.1 gives the relation. The specification yields a calibrated predictive interval; for approximately normal residuals the standard uncertainty is u₂ = (q0.95 − q0.05)/(2 × 1.645), so the assumed 20% corresponds to a calibrated 90% interval of ±33% of X_LM. For non-normal residuals the conformal interval is retained and u₂ is its half-width divided by the coverage factor of the observed distribution. The 20% is therefore a requirement on the fitted estimator and not a result, and Fig. 10a shows what is lost if the fitted estimator falls short of it.
Change: Section 3.3.1 (new paragraphs); Fig. 10a.

R2.9. Units
Response: Yes, it denotes kgf/cm². The unit is now written kgf/cm² everywhere (Abstract, Table 2, Table 10, text), with the SI equivalent given where it is introduced: 1 kgf/cm² = 0.0980665 MPa, so the envelope is 5.9–8.3 MPa.
Change: Abstract; Section 4.2; Table 2; Section 6.3; Table 10.

R2.10. Terminology
Response: Section 4.3 now defines the terms. Error is the simulated deviation from the reference (Eq. 17); bias is its mean; uncertainty is a standard uncertainty declared by the method (u), or the expanded uncertainty U = 2u; the “95% error band” is the 95th percentile of the absolute simulated error, an empirical property of the simulation and not an expanded uncertainty; accuracy is not used for simulated quantities. The text is changed accordingly (“95% uncertainty band” and “coverage band” become “95% error band”; “the accuracy decreases” becomes “the error band widens”), and the distinction between modeled uncertainty and demonstrated performance is made in the Abstract, Section 1.4, Section 6.3 (Table 10) and the Conclusions.
Change: Section 4.3; Abstract; Sections 5.4, 5.8, 6.3, 7.
